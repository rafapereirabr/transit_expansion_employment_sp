# Literature review

1. Define the review question, scope, population, outcomes, exposure, geography, period, and purpose.
   Ask only if an unresolved dimension would materially change the search.
2. Inspect locally supplied papers and notes first. When current or external literature is needed,
   search the web and prefer journal pages, working-paper repositories, author manuscripts, and
   official dataset or institutional documentation. Never upload restricted project data.
3. Search in concept families rather than one exact phrase: substantive mechanism, policy or
   exposure, outcome, empirical setting, and identification design. Record enough search detail to
   make important omissions visible.
4. For each central study, extract only what is supported by the source:
   - question, population, setting, and period;
   - estimand or target claim;
   - treatment or exposure and comparison;
   - identifying variation and maintained assumptions;
   - treatment timing, interference, sorting, and measurement treatment;
   - main finding, relevant limitation, and transferability to this project.
5. Synthesize by agreement, disagreement, design family, and unresolved identification problem.
   Separate absence of located evidence from evidence of absence; do not claim novelty from an
   incomplete search.
6. Translate the literature into design implications: which assumptions have precedent, which are
   contested, what diagnostics are customary, and what the literature does not solve here.
7. Cite every paper-specific or factual claim with a direct link. Distinguish verified facts,
   interpretations, and open questions. If saving a report, use `quality_reports/literature/`.

Return the design-relevant synthesis first, then a concise study matrix and search limitations.
